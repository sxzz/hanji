# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Readers, students, researchers, and language-tool builders comparing how a
Han character is written and catalogued across mainland China, Hong Kong,
Taiwan, Japan, and Korea. They may scan the full table, filter by meaningful
differences, or open a single character directly while working across several
interface languages.

## Product Purpose

Hanji is a fast, transparent reference for the union of five regional common
character lists. It places regional glyphs, code points, stroke counts,
frequency, readings, listing status, related forms, and primary dictionaries
side by side. Success means that a reader can verify a difference without
mistaking a font-design choice, an unlisted reference glyph, or an uncertain
relationship for an official regional standard.

## Brand Personality

Precise, neutral, and scholarly. The interface should feel like a carefully
annotated proof sheet: quiet enough for close reading, but distinctive enough
that structure and evidence remain legible at a glance.

## Anti-references

Do not present the project as an authoritative dictionary or conceal the
limits of its public sources. Avoid decorative region-coded color, flag-led
national branding, generic dashboard card grids, and effects that compete
with the glyphs. Never let the interface language choose the wrong regional
font for data.

## Design Principles

- Put glyph comparison first and supporting metadata in its service.
- Make provenance, uncertainty, and scope explicit rather than smoothing over
  inconvenient distinctions.
- Preserve regional typographic fidelity independently of interface language.
- Keep direct lookup, filtering, and returning to context fast and predictable.
- Use the visual language of a proof sheet: restrained, dense where useful,
  and calm during long reading sessions.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

The repository does not declare a formal WCAG conformance level. Existing
requirements include visible keyboard focus, reduced-motion support, readable
light and dark themes, and signals that remain understandable without color.
Grouping uses spacing and structure as its primary cue, with color only as a
redundant aid. Locale and regional font handling must remain explicit so each
reader sees the intended character form.
